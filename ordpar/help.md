# Help

## Settings

Ordpar is one dictionary that can be read in different ways. Its [settings](https://ordpar.no/settings/) decide what an article shows: which languages, how many examples, how much a small article holds, and more. A change applies at once and is kept in this browser; nothing is sent anywhere.

**Presets.** At the top of Settings, four buttons set all the article settings at once: *Default*, *English emphasized*, *Concise* and *For learners*. A preset is a starting point, not a mode: change any setting afterwards and your change stays. The page shows which preset your settings match, if any. *Default* puts the article settings back as Ordpar starts. A preset changes only the settings under [Article](https://ordpar.no/settings/#article); your style, search and offline settings stay as they are.

**The English first: English emphasized.** For when you read mostly for the English. In each definition the English is in the full text colour and the Norwegian is faded, and short formula lines, such as "used as an adverb:", show in English only. The settings behind it: *Emphasis, when both languages show*, and *Short formula lines*.

**Shorter articles: Concise.** For when you only need the meaning. Each definition shows only its English, and each sense and set phrase shows its first two examples, with a line such as "Show 12 more examples" to open the rest. Small articles leave out some subsenses and compound lists. An article not yet translated still shows its Norwegian. The settings behind it: *Definitions*, *Examples under each sense and set phrase*, and, under *Small articles*, *How much of the article it shows*.

**Practising your Norwegian: For learners.** For reading the Norwegian first and checking yourself. An eye beside the headword hides the English of every example in the article, or shows it again; while it is hidden, "Show English" under an example reveals that one. In each definition the Norwegian is in the full text colour and the English is faded, and short formula lines, such as "used as an adverb:", show in Norwegian and English. The settings behind it: *An eye button for the examples' English*, *Emphasis, when both languages show*, and *Short formula lines*. If you would rather every article open with the examples' English hidden, set *Examples* to "Norwegian, with the English hidden until revealed". It also shows each word's CEFR level in a small box after the headword, and each set phrase's after the phrase (*CEFR level after the headword*; see [CEFR levels](https://ordpar.no/help/#cefr-levels)). It puts each article's set phrases in order of their level, A1 first, too (*Order of the set phrases*).

**Norwegian only.** For reading the articles as Bokmålsordboka has them, with Ordpar's search, favourites and other features. *Show the articles in Norwegian only* hides every article's English, with nothing to click to reveal it, and puts the labels inside the article in Norwegian: its headings and buttons, its word class and its inflection tables. It sits over your other settings, which apply again as they were when you switch it off. The Norwegian is Bokmålsordboka's article as it was in the copy Ordpar works from, with our corrections, so a change made at ordbokene.no since then may not be here yet. Ordpar's own added examples stay, marked "+". With *English equivalents* hidden, there is no English beside the words in the search suggestions, lists, Recent, History and Favourites either, nor when the pointer rests on a linked word, and the "Search English words" switch goes, so the search and Random word are Norwegian; you choose whether the lists show each word's first Norwegian definition or nothing, and the same for the linked words. A button in the header switches Norwegian only on or off on every page, and this device remembers it; you can turn the button off in Settings.

**Labels in Norwegian.** *Labels inside an article*, under Article's Language settings, puts an article's headings and buttons in Norwegian, such as "Opphav" for "Etymology" and "Vis bøyning" for "Inflection", with its word class and the words of its inflection tables. With "Norwegian only" on, they are all in Norwegian whatever this setting says. The rest of the site stays in English, the buttons at the foot of an article ("Copy link", "Share") included.

**Spoken words.** The speaker icon after a headword, a definition line, an example or a form in an inflection table reads it aloud; press it again to stop. *Speed of the spoken words*, under Article, plays the recordings as recorded or at 90%, and at 90% the voice keeps its pitch. The icons need an internet connection, also with the offline copy.

The words are read by Ida, [Amazon Polly's](https://aws.amazon.com/polly/) synthetic Norwegian Bokmål voice, at 90% of her usual speed, for clarity. Where she misreads a word, the reading is corrected with a pronunciation from NB Uttale (Språkbanken) or by ear, and the listening goes on. Example sentences are read without their parentheses and explanations.

**A few more.** *Inflection tables*: "Always shown" puts a word's forms on the page without a click, handy while you learn them. *What a click on a linked word opens*: a small article on the page, or the full article. *Article layout*: "Closer to ordbokene.no", if that is the layout you know.

## Pronunciation

Written in ordinary Norwegian spelling, not phonetic symbols. When a word is not stressed on its first syllable, a mark after the stressed syllable shows where the stress falls, and which of Norwegian's two word melodies it has: ˊ for tone 1, ˋ for tone 2. The two tones can tell apart words that are otherwise said alike, such as bønder, 'farmers' (tone 1), and bønner, 'beans' (tone 2). How they sound depends on the dialect: in Eastern Norwegian, as in Oslo, tone 1 starts low on the stressed syllable and rises, while tone 2 falls on the stressed syllable before it rises. A hyphen means only part of the word is spelt out.

## CEFR levels

With *CEFR level after the headword* on, under Article in [Settings](https://ordpar.no/settings/#article) (*For learners* turns it on), a small box after the headword shows the word's level in the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages (CEFR), and a box after each set phrase in the article shows the phrase's own. The level is the lowest at which a learner of Norwegian needs the word, to understand it or to say it:

- **A1 · beginner**: yourself and your family, your home and everyday things, food and drink, numbers, prices, the time and the days.
- **A2 · elementary**: shopping, the bank, public transport and asking the way, work and free time, plans and appointments, the weather, a visit to the doctor.
- **B1 · intermediate**: daily life beyond the routine, such as a complaint or a problem on a journey, the main points of the news, your experiences, feelings, hopes and opinions.
- **B2 · upper intermediate**: abstract topics, arguments for and against, current issues, your own field and most general subjects.
- **C · advanced**: less common words, where a simpler one would do for most learners, and words whose use turns on connotation or register. C holds the framework's two highest levels, C1 and C2, together.

With the boxes on, the setting just below, *Order of the set phrases*, can show an article's set phrases by level, A1 first, instead of alphabetically; within a level they stay alphabetical.

The levels were assigned by AI (Claude, by Anthropic), article by article: each word was judged from its article in Norwegian, its definitions and examples, against the CEFR's descriptions of the levels, and a word Bokmålsordboka lists without a definition from the word itself. Each set phrase was judged on its own. C1 and C2 are shown as one level because telling them apart was the least reliable part of the judging. The way the common words were judged was checked against SVALex, a Swedish word list graded from CEFR coursebooks: 87 of 100 words came within one level of it. Take the levels as a guide, not a syllabus.
